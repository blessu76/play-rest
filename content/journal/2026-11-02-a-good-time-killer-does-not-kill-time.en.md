"Killing time" is an odd phrase. It sounds as if the minutes were an enemy and the game were the weapon. But most of us reach for a game in a spare moment for the opposite reason: we want the minutes to feel lighter, not to be destroyed. A good time-killer should leave you a little more rested than it found you.

Here are four conditions we hold our own games to. They are not a scientific checklist. They come from the games we ourselves keep and the ones we put down.

## It starts at once

A spare moment may last two minutes. If the first of them goes to signing in, accepting a prompt, or reading a long tutorial, the moment is gone before the game begins.

So a good time-killer asks for little at the start. No account to create. Rules short enough to hold in your head. [Inkisle](https://inkisle.ipe.rest) needs no account and no network, and its rules fit on one page: islands make troops, and a larger force arriving takes the island. [Sunmeld](https://sunmeld.ipe.rest) states its rules in four lines. Claimball explains a match in four lines too. If the rules do not fit on one page, the game is asking too much of a break.

## You can leave any time

A break ends when it ends, not when the game says so. The test is simple: can you close the app mid-round and lose nothing?

[Iltonggi](https://iltonggi.ipe.rest) saves your game automatically when you close it, and nothing nags you to come back every day. Inkisle works offline, so a tunnel or a lift does not interrupt it. Leaving should feel like putting a book down on the table, not like abandoning a shift.

This is also why the way a game treats absence matters. Streak counters, daily reminders and timers that punish you for stepping away all turn a pause into a small debt. We hold ourselves to not building them.

## It ends cleanly

A round with a clear end lets you stop with a clear mind. The worst endings are the ones that fade: a game that is already decided but keeps asking you to play it out, or one that always has one more thing waiting.

In Inkisle, a won round does not drag on. It ends once you are clearly ahead. Iltonggi takes 5 to 10 minutes a round. Claimball is built around short matches, and Sunmeld is a duel with a sharp finish, since whoever overflows the tub loses. (Both are still in development, so we are not quoting numbers for them.) A clean end gives you a small thing most games forget: the permission to say "that's enough for today".

## Nothing rushes you, nothing interrupts you

The last condition is about what surrounds the game. An ad that cuts in between rounds makes the break shorter and noisier. A game that keeps nudging you to hurry does the same from the inside.

Here is where our four games stand today, in the words on their own sites. Iltonggi: no ads, no tracking. Inkisle: every round free, no ads, optional help items. Sunmeld: free to try, one purchase unlocks everything, no ads. Claimball: free to try, one purchase unlocks everything, no ads, no subscription.

None of these games has been released yet. They are promises we are working toward, not claims about something you can already download, and we would rather label them that way.

## Resting, not killing

If there is a single test, it is how you feel when the game is over. Did the break get longer or shorter? Did you put the phone down, or did it keep a hand on your sleeve? A game that lets you pick it up quickly, put it down freely and finish cleanly does not kill your time. It gives you a quiet corner of it.
