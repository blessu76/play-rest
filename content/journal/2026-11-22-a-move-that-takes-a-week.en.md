Most of what we write on this journal is about short rounds. It helps to look at the opposite end once: a turn-based game where one move can take days.

## Chess by post

Before there was a screen, there was the envelope. People played chess by sending each move through the mail, and an international federation for correspondence chess, the ICCF, was founded in 1951. Earlier bodies existed before it. A game could last months, and a single position might sit on a table for a week while a player thought.

Today the same idea lives on as asynchronous play, where you make a move, close the app, and the other player answers when they can.

## What a slow clock changes

Time between moves changes the character of the game.

You can study. With days to answer, a move is a prepared decision. You can look things up, analyze at length and sleep on a hard position. The result is less about reflexes and more about depth.

You can play many games at once. Because no single game needs your attention for long, you can keep several going. A turn takes a minute, and the clock does the waiting.

You must be patient. The game does not respect your schedule. You may wait a long time for a reply, and nothing can be done about it.

## Where short sessions differ

A short session reverses almost all of that. Everything has to fit inside a break: the thinking, the move and the result. A game built for it cannot rely on deep study. It needs a position you can read quickly, a decision that does not require a notebook and a round that finishes.

That is the same trade-off we describe elsewhere in this journal, seen from the other side. The turn-based form can be slow or fast, and what changes is how much thinking the clock gives you.
