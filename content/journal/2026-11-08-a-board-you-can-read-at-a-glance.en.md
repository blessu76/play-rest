When you open a game for a few spare minutes, the board is the first thing you have to understand, and you only get a second or two to do it. Who is ahead? What is mine? What is about to change? If the answer takes a pause and a squint, a short game has already lost some of its short time.

A board that can be read at a glance is one of the quiet virtues of a good small game.

## Ownership should be visible

In a territory game, the whole picture is "who owns what". The best way to show that is to let the board itself carry it. If a tile or an island changes color when it changes hands, you do not need a score panel to know how things stand. You can see it.

This is also a way to make progress feel physical. A board that fills in with your color is a different kind of reward from a number going up.

## Claimball: a notebook grid

[Claimball](https://claimball.ipe.rest) is played on a 6 by 10 notebook grid, and your tiles and your AI rival's tiles are split by color. The tile where your ball bounces becomes your land. Hitting the ball with the center of your paddle is a smash, and a smash claims three tiles at once.

The grid is deliberately plain. A notebook has lines and squares, and everyone already knows how to read one. We think a plain background helps here, because the interesting thing on the screen should be the land changing hands, and not the decoration.

## Inkisle: color and shape

[Inkisle](https://inkisle.ipe.rest) has a related problem. Islands and fleets belong to one side or the other, and if color were the only clue, some players would struggle. Many people cannot tell certain colors apart. So each side in Inkisle has its own color and its own flag shape, and the board stays readable if the colors do not help you.

That is a small decision, and it is easy to skip. It matters because "at a glance" has to be true for everyone who opens the game, not just for the people who see colors the way the designer does.

## What a glance cannot do

We should be careful not to oversell this. A clear board shows you the situation. It does not make the decisions for you, and a readable game is not an easy game. Claimball's rival will still take land, and Inkisle's other side will still take islands. Readability only makes sure that when you lose a tile or an island, you know exactly where and why.
