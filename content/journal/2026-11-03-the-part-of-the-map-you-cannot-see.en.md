Most of us meet turn-based games through ones where everything is on the table. In chess, both armies sit in plain view, and the whole game is about what to do with what you can already see. That is a lovely shape. It is not the only one.

Other turn-based games hide part of the board. You act, and then you find out what the hidden part held. The usual word for this is fog, and it changes what a single turn feels like.

## A decision with a gap in it

When everything is visible, a hard turn is hard because several moves look good and you have to pick one. When part of the board is hidden, a hard turn is hard for a different reason: you have to choose before you know. Is the land next door empty or defended? Is it worth finding out first, or is it better to press on?

We think this suits short rounds, though that is an opinion and not a finding. A hidden square hands each turn a small question to carry, and the answer arrives a few moves later instead of an evening later. The round gets a little curiosity without getting any longer.

## How fog works in Iltonggi

[Iltonggi](https://iltonggi.ipe.rest) is a short, fast, classic territory-conquest strategy game set in Korea's Three Kingdoms era. In it, land you have not visited stays covered in fog, and only a spy can peek at it. The map works town by town, and bigger towns gather more troops, so the capital is large and the countryside is small. Even with part of the map hidden, you can reason about where the weight probably lies.

When we wrote about why we are building the game, we said we felt that a board where you press on, curious about the unknown, makes a better story than one where everything is visible at a glance. We still think so. We also know it is a taste.

## Fog is not free

Fog can go wrong. If you like to work everything out, hidden information may feel like noise. A game can also overdo it, so that you are asked to guess with nothing to guess from, and then it stops being curiosity and becomes a coin toss.

The line we try to hold is that fog should come from a rule you can read, such as land you have not visited, and not from a thumb quietly placed on the scale. You always have a way to look, because you can send a spy. Whether that balance feels like curiosity or like a chore is something we can only learn from people playing, and Iltonggi is not released yet. It is marked "coming soon", and the iPhone version comes first.

## Seeing less, deciding more

A fully visible board asks you to out-calculate the other side. A foggy one also asks you to be comfortable with a decision you cannot fully check. Neither is better. They are two different ways of spending a few minutes of attention, and a break has room for both.
