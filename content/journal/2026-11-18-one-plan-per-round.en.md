If you play turn-based games in short sittings, you will recognise a certain kind of lost round. Nothing went badly wrong. You made a reasonable move each turn, and at the end you looked at the board and could not say what you had been trying to do.

A small habit helps, and it costs almost no time: have one plan per round.

## What a plan is

A plan is not a forecast of the next ten moves. It is a sentence you could say out loud. "I will take the north first." "I will keep my side tidy and let the other player run out of room." "I will find out what is in the fog before I commit."

One sentence is enough. The point is that the next move now has something to be measured against. Of the three moves that all look fine, one moves toward the plan, and that one is a good tie-breaker.

## Why only one

Two plans compete for the same attention. In a break, attention is the one thing you do not have a lot of. With a single plan, thinking a turn costs you a single question: does this help, or does it hurt?

It also makes the round easier to learn from. If you ended with a loss, you can say whether the plan was bad or whether it was fine and your execution slipped. Without a plan, a loss is only a feeling.

## Check it every turn

A plan is a guess, and the board is allowed to disagree. The habit is not to defend the plan. It is to look at it each turn and ask whether it still makes sense.

Sometimes the answer is to drop it, and the best players do that without ceremony. The board changed, the other side did something you did not expect, or you simply learned something. Replace the sentence with a new one and carry on.

## Plans in our games

A plan looks different in each of our games. In [Iltonggi](https://iltonggi.ipe.rest), a town's size decides how many troops it gathers, so a plan can be as simple as "build up the capital first". In the fog, a plan can also be "send a spy before I choose where to strike". In [Sunmeld](https://sunmeld.ipe.rest), where whoever overflows the tub loses, a plan might be "keep the tub low and make the other side run out of room".

These are examples of how we think about the games, not strategy advice we have tested at scale. Neither game is released, and the right plan is something players will find, not us.

## A short rule

Say the plan in your head before the first move. Check it before each move. Change it when it stops helping. If you try it for a week of breaks, you may find your rounds end with less of that "what was I doing?" feeling.
