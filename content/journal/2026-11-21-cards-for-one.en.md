A deck of cards and a flat surface are enough for a whole game. That is the quiet strength of patience, the family of one-player card games most people know as solitaire.

## Two names, one idea

The same kind of game has gone by different names. In British English it is called patience and in American English solitaire. Scandinavian sources also use the name cabale. The earliest references that scholars point to come from German and Scandinavian sources in the second half of the eighteenth century. Beyond that, the history gets vague quickly, and we would rather say so than repeat a tidy origin story we cannot check.

What the names do tell us is something about the player. Patience is the virtue the game asks for, and solitaire is the condition it is played in. Both words describe the player and neither describes the cards.

## Why it keeps working

A solo card game has a few features that suit short breaks.

The rules fit in your head. Most layouts can be explained in a minute, and the cards themselves carry the information. You do not need a manual because the deck is already familiar.

It ends by itself. A layout either comes out or it gets stuck. There is no need to decide when to stop, and no game sits half-finished in a way that bothers you.

It has a built-in difficulty dial. Some versions are generous and others are strict, and the shuffle means some deals simply cannot be won. Part of the pleasure is that not every loss is your fault.

## What it gives up

A solo game with a shuffled deck leans on luck. When the deal decides a lot, skill shows mostly in how you avoid wasting a good deal. That is a gentler kind of challenge than an opponent who answers you, and some players will find it too quiet.

## What we take from it

The idea we find most useful is the first one: the game should be understandable from the pieces alone.
