Before a game has a board or a score, it has an answer to a small question: who acts now? The answer is so basic that we rarely notice it, and yet it decides how the whole game feels in your hands.

There are three common answers.

## One at a time

The oldest answer is to take turns. You act, then the other side acts, then you act again. Chess works this way, and so do most card and board games you can name.

What this gives you is a pause that belongs to you. While the other side is deciding, you can read what just changed. While you are deciding, nothing else moves. The pace is set by the players, not by the game.

[Sunmeld](https://sunmeld.ipe.rest) is built on this. You and an AI opponent drop stones into one shared tub in alternating turns, and every stone you place changes the space the other side has to work in. [Iltonggi](https://iltonggi.ipe.rest) is turn-based too, and the world waits while you choose where to strike.

## Everyone at once

A second answer is that both sides act at the same time, in secret, and the choices are revealed together. Rock, paper, scissors is the plainest example. Nobody gets to respond to anybody, because nobody has seen anything yet.

Here the whole game is a guess about the other player. There is no board to read, only a person, and that is why a game this small can still feel tense. It works as a way to settle something in seconds. It is not a way to build a position, because there is nothing left on the table afterwards.

## No turns at all

The third answer is real time. The game keeps running and you act whenever you can. [Inkisle](https://inkisle.ipe.rest) and [Claimball](https://claimball.ipe.rest) are like this: fleets keep sailing, a ball keeps bouncing, and what you do next depends on how fast you notice.

Real time gives a game energy and a feeling of being caught up in it. It also means the game, not you, decides the rhythm.

## Which one suits a break?

We do not think one answer is the right one. A break that is calm and uninterrupted suits taking turns, because you can think as long as you like. A break you spend standing up, or one that may end in a moment, may be happier with a short, lively real-time round.

What we would suggest is to ask the question before choosing a game: do I want the game to wait for me, or do I want it to move? Our games answer it in different ways on purpose, and the cards on [play.ipe.rest](https://play.ipe.rest) describe how each one plays.

None of the games mentioned here is released yet. Iltonggi and Claimball are marked "coming soon", and Inkisle and Sunmeld are in development, so this is a guide to how each is meant to be played, not a review.
